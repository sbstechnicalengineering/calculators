What is this tool?
A 3D model of a tensile test on a bolt. The bolt is pulled in a straight line until it stretches, yields, necks and breaks, so you can see how a fastener's rated strength is measured on a universal testing machine.
Getting around the tool
User guide is this page. 3D simulation is the machine, and its Guided tour button replays the walkthrough any time. Compare grades puts two bolts side by side and can load either into the machine. Best practices covers what to know when running or reading a test. Real life, not a simulation sets the model beside a real test.
Stress colour
The bolt shank turns from green (elastic, safe) through orange (yield) to red (necking and failure) as the load rises. Real steel does not change colour. This is a teaching overlay you can switch off with the Stress colour button.
Part labels
Labels appear as you zoom: the whole machine at a distance, then the load cell and fixtures, then the neck up close. Click one to fly to that part. Switch them off with Part labels.
Why this test matters
Tensile testing is how manufacturers determine a bolt's rated strength. The Sy (yield strength) and Su (ultimate tensile strength, UTS) values used throughout this tool come from tests like this one.

Best practices

Stages of a tensile test. Every bolt goes through the same sequence on its way to fracture, modelled on the classic specimen tensile-test diagram. Each specimen below is a live 3D render, and you can click one to rotate it and step through the stages. Inside the viewer, every stage before fracture also has a Section view button that cuts the bolt open to show what is happening inside. Open the 3D simulation tab to run the test and jump to any of these stages.

Setting up the machine

Pull straight along the bolt's axis
Look at the Fixtures view: the head holder and nut holder sit on the bolt's centre line, so the pull is purely axial. On a real machine, misalignment adds bending and can lower the measured strength.
Hold the head and the nut properly
A hardened flat washer sits under the head and another under the nut, and these bear on hardened slotted plates, so the load is spread over flat, hard surfaces as it is in a real test. Test standards also ask for some bare thread between the nut and the smooth part of the bolt (at least one bolt diameter in ISO 898-1), so the test measures the bolt itself.
Pick the right load cell
A load cell is the sensor that measures the pull, and it works best when the break load sits well inside its range. The machine fits the smallest standard cell with at least 25% headroom over the expected break load. Once a bolt is mounted, the Machine and specimen panel shows the choice and how much of its range you use.
The elastic stretch you see is enlarged
While it is still springy, a real bolt stretches by only a tiny fraction of a percent, far too little to see. The 3D view enlarges that springy stretch about 10 times so you can follow it. Stretch that stays after the pull is released is shown at its true size. The numbers on screen (force, stress, strain and extension) are always the true values.
Test speed matters
Real tests pull slowly and steadily, because pulling too fast can make a bolt look stronger than it really is. A longer gauge length can be pulled a little faster. The suggestion below is a simple guide of about 12% of the gauge length per minute. For an official test, follow the speed set by the test standard.

Reading the results

Yield vs. ultimate strength
Yield is where permanent stretch begins. Ultimate is the peak load just before failure. A bolt's usable working range sits below yield, with ultimate marking the true safety ceiling.
How yield strength is found
Most bolt materials bend gradually, with no obvious moment where they start to give. Stainless steel, titanium, brass, aluminum and heat-treated steels (SAE Grade 5 and 8, ISO 8.8 to 12.9) all behave this way. So engineers agree to call the point where a bolt has stretched 0.2% for good its yield strength. Compare these rounded curves with basic steels like SAE Grade 2 and ISO 4.6, which show a sharp, obvious yield point.
Necking marks the beginning of the end
Once a bolt passes ultimate strength, its cross-section starts narrowing at one point, here at the first engaged thread. Force falls from now on, while the crosshead keeps rising, and failure is a matter of when.
Not all failures look the same
A ductile fracture shows visible necking and a rough, fibrous cup-and-cone break, as in the model. A brittle fracture snaps suddenly with little stretch, often a sign of the wrong material, embrittlement or damage.
Elongation shows ductility
Elongation is how much longer the bolt gets before it breaks, shown as a percentage. It tells you how stretchy (ductile) the metal is. Switch between grades and watch how much less the high-strength ones stretch before they break.
Never reuse torque-to-yield bolts
Torque-to-yield bolts are tightened until they stretch a little for good, so they are single-use. Once a bolt has been pulled past its yield point it stays longer, and you can see this in the wider gap between the marks.
Temperature affects strength
Yield and ultimate strength both shift with temperature, and most materials weaken as they heat up. Standard tensile ratings assume room temperature unless a test states otherwise.

Hardened washers in a real test
In a real tensile test a hardened washer is placed under the head of the bolt and another between the nut and the fixture. They spread the load over a larger, flat, hard surface so the head and nut bear evenly and do not dig into the fixture. The 3D model shows both washers.

BETA

This tool is currently in beta. The simulation logic, material data, and results are directional and educational only. Results should not be used as a final engineering or sourcing decision without a formal print review and DFM analysis.

We encourage you to use the tool, explore its outputs, and share your feedback. If you notice a result that seems off, or have a suggestion to improve the logic, we want to hear from you. Your input helps us make this resource more useful for the entire SBS community.
